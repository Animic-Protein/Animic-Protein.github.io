ANÍMIC PROTEIN · A DIDACTIC GUIDE

Perceptible
difference.

It is more than something changing. A variation becomes noticeable within a relation: what we hear, remember, expect, or can do next.

An explanatory article · Anímic Protein (AP)

In one sentence: the Còdex calls perceptible difference a sensory, contextual, or relational variation between two forms, moments, or presences that some perceptual system can detect and that changes how their relation is read.

What exactly is it?

A mark can move even when no one notices. There is a physical difference between two images; for that difference to become perceptible, it must meet attention, context, memory, or expectation. It is therefore neither an isolated property of an object nor proof that everyone saw the same thing.

The Còdex separates three moments that are often conflated: a variation exists; someone or some system detects it; the variation changes how a relation is understood. The third moment does not follow automatically. A detectable difference may create no new relation and call for no action.

Where does it come from?

Within the Còdex, the concept begins with a design question: what has actually changed, and what conditions do we need in order to perceive it? Neuroscience helps us frame this question; it did not originate the concept and does not supply a single answer.

In a classic study of visual change, Rensink, O’Regan, and Clark alternated a scene with a modified version, separated by a briefly blank field. People could take time to locate large changes; a verbal cue sped detection. This shows how attention and task shape what becomes noticeable, not that perception is defective. In another study, Yabe and colleagues recorded auditory-cortex responses when tones were omitted from a repeated sequence: the brain can respond to an expected absence. A neural response to an omission, however, is not the same as consciously noticing it or finding creative meaning in it.

There is therefore no single “brain location” where perceptible difference resides. Here we are describing a distributed function: selecting, comparing, keeping a form present long enough to contrast it, and reconsidering what a variation means in its context.

How does it feel?

There is no universal feeling. Sometimes it is a gap that becomes audible only when sound returns; sometimes a figure suddenly moves into the background, a detail that “doesn’t quite fit,” or the quiet recognition that nothing has changed for us. It may also go unnoticed. Experience varies with the person, sensory channel, attention, moment, and expectation.

That is why the Còdex does not correct someone who says “I didn’t perceive it,” turn uncertainty into error, or interpret a sensation as a diagnosis. The useful question is more modest: what did you notice, under what conditions, and what remains unclear?

When does it become fertile?

The Còdex distinguishes perceptible difference—what can be detected—from fertile difference—when a change opens a relation, question, or possibility. A phantom trace can make visible an absence that continues to affect a return. None of these ideas, by itself, authorizes transformation.

A musical example: repeat D–F–A; leave out F once; then return to D–F–A. The notes on return are the same, but the gap may have changed the listening. The return is not “better,” nor necessarily different for everyone. It is an invitation to compare.

Which dispositions can support it?

We do not need to add a new organ each time we identify a cognitive function. I propose extending existing dispositions with five small operations, each supported by software:

  • ATTENTIO · orient attention: choose whether to observe form, position, or relation. The Rosa de l’Escolta already offers this orientation; the Laboratory makes it explicit.
  • RETENTIO · keep a brief reference: show a starting point, introduce an interval, and return to it. This is comparison memory for the session, not a hidden history.
  • DISCRIMEN · compare without scoring: distinguish absence, displacement, and a change in relation. The software reveals the exact variation only after the person responds.
  • QUIES / REOBSERVE · pause: allow people to stop, look again, or say “I don’t know.” The experience does not reward ending in transformation.
  • METACOGNITIO · separate observation from interpretation: first, “what did I notice?”; then, if the person chooses, “what might it mean?” The system does not answer the second question.

These names are proposed dispositions, not diagnoses or new canonical components. They extend the already approved grammar: the record documents → the experience runs → the person decides → MUTATIO transforms only when authorized → the return preserves provenance.

How does a Còdex disposition become operational?

A disposition is not an order or a button that decides for someone. It prepares conditions and keeps the threshold between perceiving and acting open:

signal → suggestedImpulse (one proposal) → human decision → authorized action → provenance

In the Laboratory the sequence is manual: starting point → interval → variation → return → description → contrast. Each step can be stopped. If the difference is unclear, REOBSERVE or QUIET is a valid path. Exporting a record creates a JSON file only after an explicit user action; it is local, reversible, and non-canonical. MUTATIO does not act here.

Sources and limits

These studies inspire two demonstration conditions—attention during visual change and expectation during auditory omission; they do not prove the Còdex’s full account. The sensorimotor account of vision is an influential theoretical proposal, not a definitive description of the brain. The Laboratory is a learning experience, not a clinical instrument or a performance measure.
